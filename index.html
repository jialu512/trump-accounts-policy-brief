Trump Accounts: Strengthen Their Impact Through Financial Literacy

Aug 19, 2026

01

Introduction

In July 2025, Trump Accounts passed the House as part of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. The program provides $1,000 in seed funding to every baby born between 2025 and 2028. Invested in low-cost equity index funds, the program aims at providing millions of American families with an opportunity to begin building wealth early in life. When the accounts mature in 18 years, the funds could help young Americans finance education, purchase homes, or build long-term savings. On July 4th, 2026, Trump Accounts officially launched at full scope and contributions began (Treasury, 2026).

Opinions diverge about this new program. Supporters praise that Trump Accounts not only serve as a savings instrument but also provide families with much-needed financial education and expand stock ownership among households who otherwise may have limited exposure to the stock market (Treasury, 2026). Critics argue that families with more wealth and financial knowledge will contribute more to Trump Accounts, which can exacerbate inequality (Russell, 2025). Some opponents also question the value-added of Trump Accounts compared to the long-established 529 plans.

This policy brief is not here to take sides. We are providing a different angle – we argue that financial literacy will help American families benefit the most from Trump Accounts, and moreover, this universally accessible new program unlocks a new opportunity for people to learn financial knowledge.

Financial literacy shouldn’t be treated as given. Only one-third of Americans can answer basic financial literacy questions, and among them, the younger adults scored the lowest (Lusardi and Streeter, 2023). Intentional and well-planned programs are needed to improve people’s financial literacy level.

02

How Financial Literacy Can Help Americans Benefit the Most from Trump Accounts

Parents are the primary drivers for Trump Accounts. The long-run success of these accounts depends largely on three key household decisions:

(1) whether to open an account,

(2) whether and how much to contribute to the account, and

(3) how to use the accumulated funds.

Evidence from existing savings programs such as Roth IRAs and 529 plans suggests that financial literacy influence each of these decisions and will be a critical factor for the success of the Trump Accounts.

Opportunity 1: financial literacy helps increase participation

Voluntary savings programs often experience relatively low participation rates. For example, fewer than 1 in 5 American adults say they have or plan to use a 529 plan (Edward Jones, 2025). Despite extensive marketing efforts, adoption remains low (Briscese, List, Liu, 2025). Similarly, when looking at retirement accounts who can be particularly advantageous for young people, only 1 in 4 American households have a Roth IRA (ICI, 2025), and the ownership among young adults was even lower (Munnell, 2024).

A growing body of research suggests that financial literacy is a critical driver of participation (Figure 2), working through two behavioral channels.

Channel (a): Understanding of compounding increases perceived value of savings

Families who don’t know about interest compounding underestimate the value of long-term savings products like Trump Accounts. Unfortunately, research by Lusardi and colleagues consistently find this lack of knowledge to be prevalent among Americans, especially the younger ones (Figure 3).

Source: Lusardi and Streeter (2023).

This knowledge gap leads to a misperception of “my savings don’t matter,” and subsequent under-contribution. For example, 61% of parents who could afford to save through a 529 plan to cover more than half of college tuition still believe their savings would be meaningless (Briscese, List, Liu, 2025).

Channel (b): Complexity and Misperception Reduce Participation, but Financial Literacy Helps Overcome the Barriers

Modern financial systems have grown more complex. Behavioral economics literature finds that the complexity of financial products discourage participation (Madrian and Shea, 2001; Choi, Laibson, Madrian, and Metrick, 2004; Benartzi and Thaler, 2004). When financial decisions involve unfamiliar concepts or too much information, many households may postpone or avoid enrolling in accounts altogether.

Financial literacy can help overcome these barriers. Research consistently finds that financially literate households are more likely to plan for retirement, hold stocks, and participate in complex savings programs (Lusardi and Mitchell, 2014, 2023;).

Opportunity 2: financial literacy helps increase contributions

Opening an account is only the first step. The long-run success of any savings programs also depends upon sustained contributions.

Experience from other programs suggests this can be challenging. For example, median contributions to Roth IRAs are only half the recommended levels. In a recent study of Illinois 529 Plan holders, though taxpayers can deduct up to $10,000 annually for single filer and $20,000 for joint filers on 529 contributions, the average contribution was only $1200 (Briscese, List, and Liu, 2025).

Is this under contribution due to financial resources or financial literacy? The authors found that financially literate account owners contribute a lot more, even after controlling for household income categories. That suggests financial literacy is a key to contributions.

Moreover, financially literate households adopt effective savings strategies and adjust contributions over time, while less financial literate households are more likely to rely on simple heuristics that lead to lower long-run balances in 529 accounts.

Opportunity 3: financial literacy can help strengthen the impact of Trump Accounts

The long-term impact of Trump Accounts will depend not only on participation and contribution, but also on whether the funds are used appropriately and optimally.

When a beneficiary turns 18, a Trump Account is generally treated as a traditional individual retirement account (IRA) and withdrawals are subject to ordinary income taxes. And just like any IRAs, withdrawals from Trump Accounts before age 59 and half are subject to a 10% penalty. But the penalty is waived for certain uses including qualified college expenses and up to $10,000 for a qualifying first-home purchases.

So, there’s a real risk of some 18-year-old’s emptying out their Trump Accounts (after paying taxes and penalty) and splurging on consumption goods. Evidences show not everyone can handle windfalls optimally. One study found that 42% of inheritance recipients had spent the entire inheritance within a year (Thompson and James, 2026).

Financial literacy can help. When beneficiaries eventually gain access to their accounts, financial literacy may help them make more informed decisions about how to use these resources for qualified purposes, while avoiding unnecessary tax costs.

03

How Trump Accounts Differ from CDAs and Baby Bonds

CDAs in the US are public funds controlled by state governments, to promote postsecondary education and other child development uses. For example, under California’s CalKIDS program, the state legislature appropriates money to the CDA program, which then allocates a specified amount to each eligible child. The money is placed in a state-owned account, invested while the child grows up. When the child becomes eligible, the program pays approved colleges or training institutions directly, and any unused funds generally remain with the program rather than goes to the family.

In the US, the “Baby Bond” idea was first proposed by academics (Hamilton and Darity, 2010) and gained some public awareness in the 2010s. Baby Bonds share some similarities with CDAs but differ in their key policy objectives and design. A CDA primarily focuses on promoting education, while Baby Bonds aim at reducing wealth inequality and generational poverty. As a result, Baby Bonds can be used beyond education, including homeownership, business formation, and retirement savings.

As of 2026, baby bonds remain largely experimental at the state level in Connecticut and California. A primary concern is fiscal cost. Critics have raised concerns about higher taxes or reallocation from other programs, and question young people’s ability to use the funds productively.

Trump Accounts are fundamentally different from a state CDA or Baby Bond in the sense that Trump Accounts are owned by individual families, who can make contributions and eventually make withdraws.

Trump Accounts, 529 Plans, traditional IRAs, and Roth IRAs are all financial accounts. Their differences mainly lie in their tax treatments (see table below).

Compared to 529 Plans, Trump Accounts provide less favorable tax treatments for education expenses; however, the new savings instruments have greater flexibility by allowing more qualified spending purposes than 529 and can transition into retirement savings vehicle for the beneficiary.

05

Experience of Asset-Building Accounts Abroad

UK Child Trust Fund (CTF)

The Child Trust Fund (CTF) was introduced in the UK in 2005 and discontinued in 2011. Every child received an initial government deposit of 250 pounds, with optional contributions from families.

To increase take-up, the government opened accounts for children whose parents didn’t sign up. As a result, 6.3 million children ultimately held CTF accounts, over 25% of which were opened by the government rather than by parents (Advanta Wealth, 2025).

The low levels of parental engagement produced two challenges: low contribution and a high percentage of lost/forgotten accounts. In 2020, when the first cohort of CTF beneficiaries turned 18, many children and families were either unaware they had a CTF or couldn’t access it. Some beneficiaries didn’t know the identity of their CTF provider as their account was sold to a different financial institution (House of Commons Library, 2025). As of 2025, about 30% of matured CTFs remained claimed. Moreover, average balances were relatively modest: about 2000 pounds, due to low contributions.

The UK experience highlights the importance of sustained engagement with families in asset-building programs. Universal savings accounts alone do not guarantee engagement. Government can set up a program to help encourage savings, but the success ultimately depends on parental awareness, involvement, and understanding of how the accounts work.

Singapore’s Baby Bonus & Child Development Accounts (CDA)

In 2001, Singapore launched the Baby Bonus Scheme and Child Development Accounts (CDA). Unlike the UK experience, the Singapore program has generated stronger engagement from parents.

A few things can be learned from the Singapore program: (1) the government provides dollar-for-dollar matching of parental contributions (up to a limit). This matching creates strong incentives for parents to save regularly for their children. (2) The Singapore baby bond accounts are integrated with the government’s administrative and banking system, so account management is straightforward and prevented the lost/forgotten accounts problem mentioned before for the UK experience; (3) CDA funds can be used for a variety of child-related expenses including childcare, preschool education, healthcare, and college education. This flexibility of how the funds can be used has kept parental engagement high; (4) the Singapore program’s rules are clearly specified and easy to understand . Their website has clear guidance on how the accounts operate and how to use the funds. The transparency of the Singapore program built trust among parents and families have provided sustained contributions over time.

Financial literacy in the US among the young and the old

As discussed above, financial literacy is an important factor affecting whether parents enroll in Trump accounts and make sustained contributions over time. However, research has shown financial literacy levels in the United States remain persistently low both among the young people and the adults.

Financial literacy among the young

PISA 2022 data show that about 17% of 15-year-old students in the US failed to reach the baseline level of proficiency in financial literacy (level 2 of 5). Often considered closely related to financial literacy, mathematics performance among students has also declined significantly from 2003 to 2022 (while reading and science performances have trended up).

The current financial education offered in school may have exposed many young people to mostly basic and limited financial concepts. As shown in Figure (4), few young people reporting having learned topics related to investing s (OECD’s 2022 PISA financial literacy assessment).

Strengthening education on investment and personal finance management may therefore help prepare future beneficiaries to make effective use of these accounts.

Financial literacy among adults

Financial literacy among US adults is also low. On average, US adults can correctly answer about half of the 28 questions measuring personal finance knowledge (GFLEC P-Fin Index 2025). Socioeconomic backgrounds are found to be correlated with financial knowledge. For instance, women have lower level of financial literacy than men, and higher income groups demonstrated more financial knowledge than lower income groups.

Most notably, young adults (Gen Z) performed especially poorly on financial literacy tests (Figure 5). Gen Z respondents scored particularly low on topics like saving, investing, and insuring, compared to older cohorts. This is alarming because Gen Z are getting to the age of marriage and starting a family. Their low levels of financial knowledge can translate to low engagement and low contribution to Trump Accounts, even if they sign up for one.

07

Conclusions and Policy Implications

Trump Accounts provides the next generation of young Americans with an opportunity to build wealth early, so that they have the resources to get education, buy home, pursue entrepreneurial dreams, and achieve other life milestones.

As a universal and large-scale asset-building program, Trump Accounts have the potential to expand asset ownership and introduce more households to long-term investing. The accounts may serve as an entry point for parents and children to learn more about investing, savings, and financial planning.

However, experience from similar savings programs remind us that access to an account is not sufficient. The long-run success of the program will depend not only on the initial enrollment but also on sustained contributions and effective use of the funds over time. Financial literacy can therefore play an important, complementary role by helping families understand the benefits of long-term saving and consistent participation.

Two practical steps could help strengthen the impact of Trump Accounts:

First, Trump Accounts could serve as a platform for delivering financial education. When parents sign up for Trump Accounts, we have a precious window of their attention. We can leverage this opportunity and provide simple educational materials and interactive tools on the program’s website.

Second, financial education efforts should meet parents where they are. Strengthening financial literacy alongside the rollout of Trump Accounts could thus increase engagement, encourage sustained contributions, and help families make effective use of the accounts over time.

Use Trump Accounts to deliver financial education: Add financial education or tools to understand the benefits of Trump Accounts on their webpage.

To meet parents where they are – Trump Accounts will be more successful with more financial literacy.
